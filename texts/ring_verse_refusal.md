# naweri — The Ring Verse, Refused

*Among people who build languages, translating Tolkien's Ring Verse is a familiar custom. Phi answers the inscription with a refusal, but refusal cannot mean bleaching the menace away. Tolkien's words remain beside the Phi passage; the surrounding stanza's lords, throne, and other Rings are discussed without reproducing the rest. The four Phi lines turn the inscription's claims into accusations and decline to make ruler or lord ordinary roles. The verse is J. R. R. Tolkien's, from* The Lord of the Rings.

---

## The attempt

> *One Ring to rule them all, One Ring to find them, / One Ring to bring them all and in the darkness bind them.*

Take it word by word, honestly.

**Ring** is sayable. `sorui` is a circle, and `thumai sorui`, finger-circle, gives the object its ordinary composition. The refusal does not begin with the metal.

**One** is `ta`. **Find** is `hekawi`; **bring** is `thola`; the adjective `nuelo`, dark, is also the regular quality noun **darkness**. Phi can say each without judging the scene in advance. Nearly the whole couplet is ordinary vocabulary, which leaves the refusal exactly where it belongs.

**All** and **them** need more care. The surrounding stanza has counted the other Rings before the inscription speaks of ruling them. The refusal follows that dominion through those Rings to the people wearing them: `theula whu phirae thumai sorui wenuha miona`, everyone who wears a different finger-ring. The plural pronoun `lo shia` then keeps hold of the same people. This turn follows the people whose choices the Ring closes; it does not claim that Tolkien's pronoun means every person.

**To rule** has no Phi root for one being's dominion over others. Phi has words for social rules, but none offers the Ring a respectable disguise. `nasholu`, rule, is a stated requirement, permission, or prohibition. `nashaku`, enforce, reports action taken in relation to such a rule. `karami`, authority, is claimed or recognised power within a social scope, and its legitimacy must be judged separately. The words `kulo`, guide, and `theluo`, steward, would falsify the line by replacing power with care. The verb `kawhera`, coerce, states how dominion closes practical choice. The refusal uses it as an accusation, not a title.

**Lord** and **throne** belong to the surrounding stanza rather than the quoted inscription, but they face the same refusal. Phi can name who holds `karami` and then ask where that authority came from, how far it reaches, and whether it is legitimate. It does not turn authority into the person's nature. `meipa` covers anything made for sitting, even an exalted or reserved seat. The furniture may be raised; its occupant's dominion still needs an honest clause.

**Bind** is the subtle one. The word `nolami`, bond, is mutual connection and cannot carry magical subjugation. The verb `tiwa`, tie with a flexible line, is physical and transitive. The final line stretches that concrete fastening into metaphor. It does not call captivity a bond.

## What comes out instead

The source gives four infinitive purposes. The refusal changes them into four present-tense accusations. It does not translate the offices of domination; it identifies the acts beneath them:

```
ta thumai sorui theula whu phirae thumai sorui wenuha miona kawhera.
one finger circle UNIV REL different finger circle wear person coerce.
(One finger-ring coerces everyone who wears a different finger-ring.)

ta thumai sorui lo shia hekawi.
one finger circle PL 3SG find.
(One finger-ring finds them.)

ta thumai sorui theula whu phirae thumai sorui wenuha miona thola.
one finger circle UNIV REL different finger circle wear person bring.
(One finger-ring brings everyone who wears a different finger-ring.)

ta thumai sorui mua nuelo lo shia tiwa.
one finger circle LOC dark PL 3SG tie.
(One finger-ring ties them in the darkness.)
```

The first line names the people who wear the other Rings. `lo shia` returns to them in the second and fourth lines, while the third names the whole group again where the source repeats **all**. The menace stays. `kawhera` makes the first line an account of constrained choice, while `tiwa` makes the last line bodily and uncomfortable. Neither word grants the Ring a legitimate office.

## Why this is the entry

A language cannot stop anyone from doing harm, and a missing root is not a wall. Phi can testify about domination through `kawhera`, coercion, while the exact source terms remain beside the passage. What it declines is a neutral person-role for ruling others. The verb `tiwa` separates imposed fastening from `nolami`, the bond people form with one another.

The refusal is therefore narrower than silence. The Ring is present, its acts are audible, and no title makes them clean.
