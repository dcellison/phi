# loa — On Giving

*A person with many possessions asks about giving. Gibran answers with a prudent dog, a full well, an orchard, and gifts that become wings. The Phi translation keeps the whole teaching, including its pain, deservingness, and divine parentage.*

*Each passage keeps four things together: the Phi sentence, its exact lexicon gloss, an English reading derived from Phi alone, and Gibran's source wording. A continuous Phi reading and a table of translation limits follow.*

---

## Translation

This translation follows the whole teaching, from the rich man's request to the freehearted earth and God. Phi describes wealth, baptism, myrtle, and God beside Gibran's exact wording. Two module words keep recurring distinctions direct: `parelu` names a judgement of deservingness without confusing it with inherent worth, while `thaweno` names a reward without turning it into a gift or agreed exchange.

**Notes:** The rich man is a person who holds abundant things; `simoe` belongs to the richness of soil, color, flavor, and similar depth, not to a rank made from possessions. Commons `phenori` states ownership, while `phelu` keeps its ordinary sense of holding. The dog receives its full itinerary, because Gibran plainly took the trouble to give it one: it hides bones in sand that holds no path while following people towards a large sacred village. The final questions preserve Gibran's identification of fear with need and thirst. Phi reports the claim without treating fear as evidence.

### The givers and their motives

```

**Notes:** Recognition, hidden desire, pain, and virtue remain separate from the gift. `thaweno` gives reward its own relation, while baptism becomes a sacred water ceremony beside Gibran's exact word. The myrtle is a shrub. It breathes in the valley, then releases its good scent into space, a small botanical liberty Gibran had already taken first. God is a sacred spirit in Phi; the source retains the identity and pronoun.

**Notes:** Phi has no general request verb. The listener hears the whole question, "Can you give?", while `whuo ha haolu` marks giving without those words. ```

**Notes:** `parelu` reports a judgement of desert; `rolia` keeps worth independent of it. The orchard and pasture repeat the proposition their trees and sheep reject, so "say not so" does not have to lean on memory alone. The ocean, stream, and cup remain present. So do the opened chest and pride. A chest can be split without Phi acquiring a word whose main employment would be rending chests. The final instruction asks the listener to observe whether they deserve to be a giving person and a tool of giving.

### Life, gratitude, and wings

```

**Notes:** Life gives to Life while the human who thinks themself the giver remains an observer. Receivers take up no weight of gratitude, lest they place a neck-frame on themselves and the giver. The giver's parent relations are gender-neutral in Phi: freehearted earth and sacred spirit. Because the reason must precede the instruction under modifier-first order, the gifts become like wings before the reader is told to rise upon them.

## Translation limits

| Source concept | Phi treatment | Boundary retained |
|---|---|---|
| rich man | a person who holds abundant things | Phi describes what the person holds without creating a rank from possessions. |
| possessions and coffer | things within an ownership relation; a storage container | Exact property rules and the precise object remain with the source. |
| overprudent dog and pilgrims | an excessively careful dog; people journeying to a sacred large village | Phi keeps the pathless sand, hidden bones, following, and destination; the inherited identities of pilgrimage and holy city remain in Gibran's line. |
| reward and deservingness | `thaweno`; `parelu` | Reward remains distinct from gift and exchange. Desert remains a judgement rather than inherent worth or a right. |
| baptism | a sacred water ceremony | Gibran's inherited rite and its theological force stay exact in the source wording. |
| myrtle | a shrub that breathes and releases its scent | The source preserves the species name; Phi preserves its valley, breath, scent, and movement into space. |
| God | a sacred spirit and parent | The source supplies the divine identity and pronoun. |
| inheritors | descendants | Phi keeps the family relation without supplying an exact law of succession. |
| naked worth and unabashed pride | worth without a cover; pride without shame; chests that must be split or disclosed | The physical and emotional images stay present without turning their English phrasing into new Phi roots. |
| debt and yoke | obligation; a frame placed on the neck | The source keeps the accounting sense of debt and the inherited name of the restraint. |
