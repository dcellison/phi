# phomila — On Children

*A person holding a baby asks Gibran to speak of children. The close Phi translation keeps his answer whole, including the bow drawn at its end. Beside it, the transmutation lets the child travel as seed without pretending that Gibran chose the same image.*

*Both renderings use four-line blocks: the Phi sentence, its exact lexicon gloss, a literal English back-translation, and Gibran's source wording. The comparison shows where the two methods part company.*

---

## Close translation

The excerpt is the teaching entire, from the person holding a baby to the bow that is stable. The close translation leaves Gibran's paradox whole and follows him into the weapon metaphor. The transmutation turns that final passage toward growing things without pretending that Gibran did the same.

### The request

```
ta himo [whu nia miso pharoe ta ru limu lopia phelu] miona sha no wei lo mia nia lo phomila haolu sho to haolu.
one HUM.CLF [REL ON REFL chest one INTS young child hold] person QUOT.COMP IMP DAT PL 1SG ON PL offspring speak QUOT.COMP.CLOSE PST speak.
(A person who held one very young child against their chest said, "Speak to us about offspring.")
gibran: "And a woman who held a babe against her bosom said, Speak to us of _Children_."

phirae miona to haolu.
different person PST speak.
(A different person spoke.)
gibran: "And he said:"
```

**Notes:** Phi does not mark woman or man as grammatical classes. The first speaker is therefore `miona`, person, and the second is `phirae miona`, a different person, so the two gender-neutral references do not collapse into one. `lopia` names the baby by life stage; `phomila` names children by descent in the request and throughout the teaching. A grown child does not return to childhood whenever a parent enters the sentence.

### Children and ownership

```
thia lo phomila thia lo phomila ma nai.
2SG PL offspring 2SG PL offspring NEG be.
(Your offspring are not your offspring.)
gibran: "Your children are not your children."

lo shia lioru wei miso wilao phomila nai.
PL 3SG life DAT REFL long-for offspring be.
(They are the offspring of Life's longing for itself.)
gibran: "They are the sons and daughters of Life’s longing for itself."

lo shia thue thia shua. whekai lo shia lue thia ma shua.
PL 3SG THROUGH 2SG come. CONTR PL 3SG ABL 2SG NEG come.
(They come through you. However, they do not come from you.)
gibran: "They come through you but not from you,"

shai lo shia nua thia nai. lo shia mua thia phenori ma nai.
CONC PL 3SG COM 2SG be. PL 3SG LOC 2SG ownership NEG be.
(Although they are with you, they are not within your ownership.)
gibran: "And though they are with you yet they belong not to you."
```

**Notes:** The first line repeats `phomila`, offspring, on both sides of the copula. It leaves Gibran's paradox intact instead of explaining it as possession before he does. The word keeps the direct descent relation without turning every son or daughter into a person still in childhood; gender remains unstated. `lioru` names Life as a condition and course, while `wilao` supplies its longing. The last line names belonging through `phenori`, ownership, a social relation wider than physical holding.

### Love, thought, body, and soul

```
thia wei lo shia thia lothea po loa.
2SG DAT PL 3SG 2SG love POT give.
(You may give them your love.)
gibran: "You may give them your love"

lo shia miso lo remo phelu. thelao thia wei lo shia thia lo remo po ma loa.
PL 3SG REFL PL think hold. CONS 2SG DAT PL 3SG 2SG PL think POT NEG give.
(They hold their own thoughts. Therefore, you cannot give them your thoughts.)
gibran: "but not your thoughts, For they have their own thoughts."

thia lo shia weloni ka po menui.
2SG PL 3SG body CAUS POT dwell.
(You may house their bodies.)
gibran: "You may house their bodies"

lo shia norea mua wireo womu menui. thelao thia lo shia norea ka po ma menui.
PL 3SG soul LOC future home dwell. CONS 2SG PL 3SG soul CAUS POT NEG dwell.
(Their souls dwell in the home of the future. Therefore, you cannot house their souls.)
gibran: "but not their souls, For their souls dwell in the house of tomorrow,"

thia ha womu po ma phemiru. we mua thia whemura thia ha womu po ma phemiru.
2SG PROX home POT NEG visit. ALSO LOC 2SG dream 2SG PROX home POT NEG visit.
(You cannot visit this home. Within your dreams too, you cannot visit it.)
gibran: "which you cannot visit, not even in your dreams."
```

**Notes:** Gibran lets love and thought travel by the same verb. The possessive stays with each: `thia lothea`, your love, and `thia lo remo`, your thoughts. Gibran places each reason after its conclusion. Phi's modifier-first order will not. The reason comes first and `thelao`, therefore, carries the reader back to the conclusion. `ka menui`, cause to dwell, lets English house remain an action without turning the noun for home into a verb. `wireo womu` makes the future house a place, while `phemiru` keeps visiting distinct from merely being there.

### Becoming and time

```
lila thia phea lo shia nai thia po meloa.
PURP 2SG AS PL 3SG be 2SG POT persevere.
(You may persevere so that you are like them.)
gibran: "You may strive to be like them,"

lioru wea luera ma wepu. shia nua luera philo ma manolu. no thelao tha thia lo shia phea thia ka kelu tho ma mesatu.
life TOWARD past NEG go. 3SG COM past day NEG stay. IMP CONS DECL.COMP 2SG PL 3SG AS 2SG CAUS become DECL.COMP.CLOSE NEG try.
(Life does not go toward the past. It does not stay with yesterday. Therefore, do not try to make them become like you.)
gibran: "but seek not to make them like you. For life goes not backward nor tarries with yesterday."
```

**Notes:** `meloa`, persevere, gives striving its sustained effort through difficulty, while `nai` makes likeness the intended state rather than a change demanded of the parent. For "seek not," ordinary `mesatu`, try, tells the parent not to attempt the conformity. The closed complement contains the whole action under that warning. `luera philo`, the past day, is Phi's established compound for yesterday. Here too, the source gives the reason after the advice and Phi gives it before.

### Bow, arrow, and archer

```
thia lo [whu nira phelu] luwi tenoa nai. thia lo phomila phea lo [whu wapi] lima tekari tiso thena lue ha lo tenoa se sepho.
2SG PL [REL cord hold] flexible tool be. 2SG PL offspring AS PL [REL fly] alive straight sharp thing ABL PROX PL tool PASS send.
(You are flexible tools that hold a cord. Your offspring are sent from these tools as living, straight, sharp things that fly.)
gibran: "You are the bows from which your children as living arrows are sent forth."

mua [whu mawha lumae phelu] ruela [whu roe ha lo tenoa ha lo thena sepho] miona [whu ha lo thena wea wapi] siru nila.
LOC [REL NONE end hold] path [REL INS PROX PL tool PROX PL thing send] person [REL PROX PL thing TOWARD fly] marker see.
(On a path that holds no end, a person who sends these things with these tools sees a marker toward which they fly.)
gibran: "The archer sees the mark upon the path of the infinite,"

lila shia lo [whu wapi] lima tekari tiso thena reshi nela wuero wapi shia roe shia puro thia luwi ka kelu.
PURP 3SG PL [REL fly] alive straight sharp thing fast COORD far fly 3SG INS 3SG strength 2SG flexible CAUS become.
(With their strength, they make you bend so that their living, straight, sharp things fly fast and far.)
gibran: "and He bends you with His might that His arrows may go swift and far."

su mua shia manuwe thia roe siora luwi kelu.
OPT LOC 3SG hand 2SG INS joy flexible become.
(May you bend with joy in their hand.)
gibran: "Let your bending in the Archer’s hand be for gladness;"

shia [whu wapi] lima tekari tiso thena lothea. shia we mureo [whu nira phelu] luwi tenoa senao keiro lothea.
3SG [REL fly] alive straight sharp thing love. 3SG ALSO stable [REL cord hold] flexible tool same way love.
(They love the living, straight, sharp thing that flies. They also love the stable flexible cord-holding tool in the same way.)
gibran: "For even as he loves the arrow that flies, so He loves also the bow that is stable."
```

**Notes:** Phi has no bow, arrow, or archer root. The close translation therefore gives each object a visible body. A bow is a flexible cord-holding tool. An arrow is a straight, sharp thing that flies, alive because Gibran calls it so. The archer is the person who sends these things with the tool; plain action would leave that relation unnamed. The marker is the place towards which they fly. `puro`, strength, supplies the archer's might; the `lila` frame gives the purpose before the bending; and the path of the infinite becomes a path that holds no end. At the end, `senao keiro`, same way, carries the source's "even as ... so" parallel. Gibran's weapon terms and capitalised divine pronouns remain exact in the source lines.

### Complete translation

```
lo phomila

ta himo [whu nia miso pharoe ta ru limu lopia phelu] miona sha no wei lo mia nia lo phomila haolu sho to haolu. phirae miona to haolu.

thia lo phomila thia lo phomila ma nai. lo shia lioru wei miso wilao phomila nai. lo shia thue thia shua. whekai lo shia lue thia ma shua. shai lo shia nua thia nai. lo shia mua thia phenori ma nai.

thia wei lo shia thia lothea po loa. lo shia miso lo remo phelu. thelao thia wei lo shia thia lo remo po ma loa. thia lo shia weloni ka po menui. lo shia norea mua wireo womu menui. thelao thia lo shia norea ka po ma menui. thia ha womu po ma phemiru. we mua thia whemura thia ha womu po ma phemiru.

lila thia phea lo shia nai thia po meloa. lioru wea luera ma wepu. shia nua luera philo ma manolu. no thelao tha thia lo shia phea thia ka kelu tho ma mesatu.

thia lo [whu nira phelu] luwi tenoa nai. thia lo phomila phea lo [whu wapi] lima tekari tiso thena lue ha lo tenoa se sepho. mua [whu mawha lumae phelu] ruela [whu roe ha lo tenoa ha lo thena sepho] miona [whu ha lo thena wea wapi] siru nila. lila shia lo [whu wapi] lima tekari tiso thena reshi nela wuero wapi shia roe shia puro thia luwi ka kelu. su mua shia manuwe thia roe siora luwi kelu. shia [whu wapi] lima tekari tiso thena lothea. shia we mureo [whu nira phelu] luwi tenoa senao keiro lothea.
```

### Translation limits

| Gibran wording | Phi treatment | Remaining difference |
|---|---|---|
| woman with a babe; he | a person holding a very young child; a different person | Phi leaves gender unstated. Calling the respondent different prevents the two third-person references from collapsing into one. Bosom becomes the more anatomical chest. |
| your children are not your children | `thia lo phomila` repeated on both sides of `nai` | The same descent phrase appears twice. Gibran later reveals that the second "your" denies ownership rather than kinship. |
| sons and daughters | `lo phomila`, offspring | Direct descent remains without assigning a gender or implying that every offspring is still in childhood. |
| Life | noun `lioru`, life, later resumed by `shia` | Phi can personify a lived condition and course, but the source alone gives Life a capital letter and theological presence. |
| belong not to you | outside `thia phenori`, your ownership | Ownership states the relevant social relation more explicitly than Gibran's broad belonging verb. |
| house their bodies | causative `ka menui`, cause to dwell | Phi treats housing as an act without making the noun for home behave as a verb. |
| not even in your dreams | additive `we` on the dream phrase | Phi makes the dream another impossible place of visiting but does not add the scalar emphasis carried by even. |
| strive; seek not | `meloa`, persevere; imperative `mesatu`, try | Perseverance stresses continued effort. Trying reports the attempted conformity without reproducing the searching motion of seek. |
| reason stated after its conclusion | reason first, followed by `thelao`, therefore | Modifier-first order moves each reason before its conclusion. The source citation keeps Gibran's order visible. |
| backward; yesterday | toward `luera`, the past; `luera philo`, past day | Phi gives backward a temporal destination rather than a directional adverb. |
| bow; arrow; archer | a flexible cord-holding tool; a living straight sharp thing that flies; the person who sends the latter with the former | The descriptions reconstruct the scene but do not replace the three archery terms as reusable vocabulary. |
| mark; infinite | a marker towards which the flying things move; a path that holds no end | The relative clause makes the target's role explicit. No-end path describes infinity without creating an abstract root. |
| bends you with His might | caused flexible becoming through `puro`, strength | `luwi kelu` covers bending without naming the drawn curve or the tension stored in the bow. |
| even as ... so | first love stated, then the second performed in `senao keiro`, the same way | The relation remains explicit, though Phi unfolds the balanced English conjunction across two sentences. |
| He; His | `shia`, third person | Phi marks neither gender nor reverential capitalization. Gibran's typography remains visible in the source lines. |

```
thia ra po ma nai. we mua thia whemura thia ra po ma nai.

thia phea lo shia po kelu.
2SG AS PL 3SG POT become.
(You may become like them.)

lioru ma turema. shia nua luera philo ma manolu. no thelao lo shia phea thia ka ma kelu.

gibran: "but seek not to make them like you. For life goes not backward nor tarries with yesterday."

thia shiro nai. thia lo phomila lima thinoe nai.
2SG tree be. 2SG PL offspring alive seed be.
(You are the tree; your offspring are living seeds.)
gibran: "You are the bows from which your children as living arrows are sent forth."

howeli lo thinoe kau wireo kolua.
wind PL seed ALL future carry.
(The wind carries the seeds toward tomorrow.)
gibran: "The archer sees the mark upon the path of the infinite, and He bends you with His might that His arrows may go swift and far."

su thia mua howeli roe siora luwi kelu.
OPT 2SG LOC wind INS joy flexible become.
(May your bending in the wind be for gladness.)
gibran: "Let your bending in the Archer’s hand be for gladness;"

lioru [whu wapi] thinoe lothea. lioru we [whu mureo nai] shiro senao keiro lothea.
life [REL fly] seed love. life ALSO [REL stable be] tree same way love.
(Life loves the seed that flies. Life also loves the stable tree in the same way.)
gibran: "For even as he loves the arrow that flies, so He loves also the bow that is stable."
```

**Notes:** Gibran's sons and daughters become `lo phomila`, offspring, so descent stays while the gendered pairing does not. "Not your children" becomes not your things, `thia lo thena ma nai`; the grammar brings the ownership claim forward. Life's longing is `lioru wei miso wilao`, and the prepositions keep the harder distinction: they come `thue` you, through you, but not `lue` you, from you. Housing is the causative of dwelling, `ka menui`, possible for bodies and refused for souls after the reason has been heard. The bow passage makes the section a transmutation rather than a translation. Bow, arrow, and archer become tree, living seed, and wind. `lima` makes the seed alive rather than treating living as its activity. The tree stays while the seed travels, and its bending in the wind expresses Gibran's gladness without turning parenthood into aim and release from a weapon. `senao keiro` keeps the final loves alike.

## What changes between the two renderings

The first part of the teaching has much the same skeleton in both renderings. A parent cannot supply a child's thoughts or house their soul. Their future cannot be made to conform. At the bow passage, the methods separate. Translation keeps the bow visible as a flexible cord-holding tool and the arrow as a straight, sharp thing in flight. Transmutation changes the scene itself. The parent becomes a tree, and the child travels as seed in wind that has no target.

### Solarpunk values

The close translation sends a child toward a mark chosen by another hand. It does not turn that image into Phi's advice about parenting; it lets Gibran make the claim. The transmutation moves the future out of the archer's sight. A seed still comes from a living parent, but wind and soil take part in what it becomes.

### Secular Buddhist philosophy

Both versions refuse ownership and mental inheritance. The close translation also includes Gibran's souls and personified Life because they belong to the source, even though Phi does not need either claim for its own account of a child. The transmutation's tree and seed make continuity visible without requiring sameness.

### Art Nouveau aesthetics

Gibran's bow already has a long curve held under tension, and the arrow gives that curve a straight line of release. The close translation sets cord and flexibility against straightness and flight. Transmutation lets the curve continue as a trunk bending in wind, with the seed's path left open.

### Peace linguistics

The close translation names the source's weapon through description and states the archer's strength without laundering it into guidance. Accuracy is not approval. Transmutation can make the ethical move that translation cannot: it removes the target and gives the child's motion to wind rather than force from a hand.

### Pre-industrial wisdom

A bow is a hand-built tool of cord and flexible material, and its presence is older than industrial violence. The close translation shows that craft alongside the purpose for which the tool is drawn. The transmutation chooses another old knowledge. Trees bend and seeds travel; the life that follows is beyond the maker's control.

Translation lets the archer draw the bow. Transmutation gives the child no target at all: the seed enters tomorrow on the wind.
