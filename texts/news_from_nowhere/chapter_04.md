# nophi lue mawha lokue — News from Nowhere, ch. 4: A Market by the Way

*The carriage leaves the river and enters a Hammersmith the narrator can scarcely recognise. A buried stream has returned to daylight, fields have replaced King Street, and a market stands among buildings whose beauty sends his heart a little higher than dignity strictly requires. Guide, `ne kulo`, is pleased by that response, not least because he made the theatre doors. The market becomes `wisola lokue`, exchange-place: food changes hands there, but money, prices, buying, and selling do not enter the Phi account.*

*Morris then lets one English syllable do two incompatible jobs. Guest, `ne phemi`, asks about the "poor"; Guide hears "poorly" and looks for illness. Phi cannot inherit that homonym by pretending. Instead, Guest reaches for `huwa`, weak, and neglects to say in what respect. Guide hears bodily weakness. Only on his second attempt does Guest name insufficient resources and rough clothes. Their confusion survives, but the words that cause it are honestly Phi's own. Each block carries the Phi passage, its word-by-word gloss, a back-translation, and the exact Morris wording beside it.*

shelami miona whuo kupe lo siora to mishoa.

```

**Notes:** the road follows Morris's recognition one clue at a time. The river gives way to the vanished street and the new fields. Then the restored creek opens onto bridge, tide, and boats; houses enter only after the water has had its say. The culvert is a `phou muila phialu shoeka`, a below-earth water-route, so the stream can be released without acquiring a piece of Victorian civil-engineering terminology. Brick uses the registered `mueri kerou`, clay-stone. Morris supplies plaster, while Phi reports the smooth pale material Guest can see. His numbered century likewise stays in the source; Phi carries the old society needed for the feeling. Morris marks women as especially well dressed and beautiful. Phi preserves the unequal distribution, some people brighter than the rest, without creating a gender class to name them. Dignity belongs to their expressions rather than rank, and open joy is joy shown without hiding.

(This style mixed the best qualities of three ancient styles.)

shia ta senalu kire to ma phelu.

mua ronua ruela phanoi whu wi shao wi moru phanoi phelu womu to whano.

shia raelu toru to phelu.

(It had a high roof.)
morris: "with a high roof,"

shia kire phea wuero silawo thorea womu kire to nai.

(Its outline was like that of a ceremonial building in a distant city.)
morris: "not unlike the Baptistry at Florence in outline,"

roa womu mulu toru to whano.
AROUND home low roof PST stand.
(A low roof surrounded the building.)
morris: "except that it was surrounded by a lean-to"

phou toru kerou loriphi thalo ruela to whano.
BELOW roof stone rainbow walk path PST stand.
(Beneath that roof stood a walking path of stone arches.)
morris: "that clearly made an arcade or cloisters to it:"

we ha womu to se ru phiro weshapi.
ALSO PROX home PST PASS INTS elegant decorate.
(This building too was decorated with great delicacy.)
morris: "it also was most delicately ornamented."
```

**Notes:** Broadway is recognisable because the roads still know where to meet. Courts are the open spaces between the buildings. The lead roof continues chapter 3's `tumoa keluo`, heavy metal; Morris keeps the exact metal. Buttresses are strong supporting wall-sections. Morris gives the building three architectural pedigrees and, sensibly, makes the narrator decline to choose among them. Phi keeps the relation: three old styles contribute their best qualities, but the building copies none. The octagon counts its eight wall-sections in ternary, `wi shao wi`. Florence belongs to Morris's sentence; Phi compares the outline with a distant city's ceremonial building. At ground level, arcade and cloister become a path beneath a low roof, carried by stone rainbows.

## wisola lokue — The exchange-place

```
lo mia lue lo loshi kosha to shua. ha lo womu neo lo mia to pakiro rihe.
PL 1SG ABL PL joyful field PST come. PROX PL home FRONT PL 1SG PST sudden rise.

(Before this, I had never felt such joy.)
morris: "that I was exhilarated to a pitch that I had never yet reached."

(I laughed with pleasure.)

mua lo rolutha mioru wolu miona nela lo lopia to meilo. lo shia keru wethalu to wenuha.
LOC PL wagon beautiful healthy person COORD PL child PST sit. PL 3SG bright garment PST wear.
(Beautiful, healthy-looking people and children sat in the wagons, and they wore bright clothes.)
morris: "wherein sat handsome healthy-looking people, men, women, and children very gaily dressed,"

lo rolutha wisola rolutha to nuwi nai.
PL wagon exchange wagon PST clear be.
(The wagons were plainly exchange-wagons.)
morris: "and which were clearly market carts,"

lo rolutha roe lorua kosha nuora pheno to nai. mia lo nuora nuola to rinu.
PL wagon INS abundant field food full PST be. 1SG PL food eat PST want.
(The wagons were full of abundant food from the fields, and I wanted to eat it.)
morris: "as they were full of very tempting-looking country produce."
```

**Notes:** the architecture catches Guest without warning, so "beautiful" cannot carry his whole response by itself. The buildings express generous making and abundant life; he feels a new height of joy and chuckles aloud. Their wagons are `wisola rolutha`, exchange-wagons, and their repeated use settles `wisola lokue`, exchange-place. The noun use of `wisola` comes from the event-noun rule. Exchange names the event without presuming currency or sale. Morris calls the food tempting-looking; Guest admits the practical result and wants to eat it. Phi sees adults and children together, while Morris's division into men and women stays in the source.

**Notes:** `wisola lokue` lets Guide call the market ordinary even while Guest is gawping at the roof. The winter Mote-House is the hall where the community meets; summer moves the meeting into fields beside the river, with Barn Elms retained through chapter 3's `ra welamu lokue`. Theatre becomes `shela wile lokue`, art-play-place, which is narrow enough not to turn every gallery or workshop into a theatre. The doors use the registered `welotu keluo`, mixed metal, for bronze, and the visible damascening becomes bright metal threads set carefully into their surface. Dick's blush is small and bodily. Morris's "girls" and "woman" remain in the citations. Phi follows the narrator's eye without turning sex into a person class: a tall person with dark hair, pale skin, a pale-green garment, and a smile notably warmer for Guide. The final "selling" remains Morris's interpretation. Phi gives the market the agents it can actually name, traders exchanging things.

## What the transmutation changed

*The chapter follows the whole journey through Hammersmith. King Street has vanished; the creek is free; the tide carries boats. Houses, gardens, bright clothes, and meeting roads lead into the market, and the carriage leaves eastward. The architecture is not reduced to "beautiful buildings." The reader can still inspect its roof and supports, count the octagon, walk beneath the outer arches, and stop with Guest at Dick's inlaid doors.*

*The exact cultural identities stay beside Phi where they are doing exact work. Morris supplies Hammersmith, King Street, Barn Elms, and Florence. He also supplies the architectural traditions and the numbered century. Phi describes each referent's role or visible substance instead of quietly substituting a vague invented name. Ternary handles the octagon as `wi shao wi` wall-sections; registered compounds supply brick, alloy, gate, and east. This chapter coins no root.*

*The market is an exchange-place, `wisola lokue`. That expression is not a polite disguise for a shop. People bring food, gather, exchange, talk, and return on busier days. Guest's expectation of sellers remains in Morris's line and appears in Phi as his search for traders exchanging things. Money, price, buying, and sale never become hidden assumptions.*

*Morris repeatedly sorts the crowd as men, women, and girls. Phi does not reproduce those person classes, but neither does it turn the crowd grey. The clothes remain bright, the bodies healthy, and the passing stranger's hair, skin, pale-green dress, and warmer smile for Guide are all there. In the regional passage, roughness belongs to terrain and clothes. Weather marks faces; it does not lower anyone's human standing. Families connect regions without making people into crosses of stock.*

*The largest change is the final pun. "Poor" and "poorly" belong to English, and a Phi homonym manufactured for one scene would be a cheat. The new misunderstanding starts when Guest calls the absent people `huwa`, weak, and leaves the relevant pressure unnamed. Guide hears bodily weakness and describes people resting at home. Guest then names resources; Guide remains baffled. The joke changes its machinery, while Morris's social discovery lands in the same place: Guest expects deprivation to be visible, and Guide does not know the category he is trying to name.*
