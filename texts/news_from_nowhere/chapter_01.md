# nophi lue mawha lokue — News from Nowhere, ch. 1: Discussion and Bed

*William Morris opens News from Nowhere in a political meeting full of interruption, conviction, and familiar bad temper. The narrator leaves by underground railway, walks home through a winter night, lies awake with his failures, and finally sleeps into the book's future England. This translation follows the whole chapter. The narrator says plainly that a friend spoke; reportative `ti` marks the story's events as secondhand wherever "says our friend" interrupts the telling, until Morris hands the story to the first person. The optative `su` holds the wish that turns the chapter: if only he could see one day of the society they argued about.*

*Each block below carries four lines: the Phi sentence, its word-by-word gloss, an English rendering derived from the Phi, and Morris's own wording. The two English lines make gains and limits visible without asking the reader to take either on trust.*

---

## The report

```
melu tha reshi thorelu shareo mua ta shero mua shalimo to ki nai tho haolu.
friend DECL.COMP fast converse discuss LOC one night LOC alliance PST PFV be DECL.COMP.CLOSE speak.
(A friend says that one night at the alliance there had been a brisk conversational discussion.)
morris: "Up at the League, says a friend, there had been one night a brisk conversational discussion,"

mua punoa moluki wireo philo hina so po kelu.
LOC society transform future day what FUT POT become.
(What may come to be on the morrow of society's transformation?)
morris: "as to what would happen on the Morrow of the Revolution,"

soli phirae melu mua shareo lumae lo newu seroli punoa wireo remo to ru kema sherewa.
SOME different friend LOC discuss end PL new mature society future think PST INTS strong claim.
(At the end of the discussion, some other friends very forcefully put forward their thoughts on the future of the new, mature society.)
morris: "finally shading off into a vigorous statement by various friends of their views on the future of the fully-developed new society."
```

**Notes:** the opening frame is the narrator's own plain claim, since he heard the friend speak; everything inside `tha ... tho` arrives as the friend's account, and neither level needs a source particle. Both `mua shalimo`, at the alliance, and `mua ta shero`, on one night, belong to the discussion being reported. Phi does not add a listener whom Morris never names.

"Up at the League" refers to a continuing association, so it takes `shalimo`, alliance. A meeting, `lona`, would name only the event. Existing `reshi` and `moluki` supply the brisk pace and the social transformation.

Morris sets the scene "one night," which becomes the plain temporal adjunct `ta shero`. His "there had been" asks for completion at a past reference point, `to ki` rather than bare `to`.

"The Morrow of the Revolution" is a particular next day rather than a vague future. The phrase `wireo philo`, tomorrow, belongs to `punoa moluki`, society's transformation. The standalone `hina` turns the disputed topic into a direct question, `so po` makes the future possible rather than certain, and `kelu`, become, lets the event happen without requiring a dedicated English-shaped verb.

The discussion is `reshi thorelu shareo`, brisk ordinary conversation rather than formal dialogue. At the end, `soli phirae melu` makes the contributors various rather than merely few, and `ru kema sherewa` gives their statements Morris's vigour. Their subject is packed into `newu seroli punoa wireo remo`: thoughts about the future of the new, fully developed society.

No discourse marker fits Morris's "finally," which marks culmination rather than cause, contrast, addition, or example. The temporal phrase `mua shareo lumae` supplies the turn instead.

### shalimo lona — The alliance meeting

```

(Although the matter under discussion was the transformation of society, the discussion reportedly stayed cordial.)
morris: "Says our friend: Considering the subject, the discussion was good-tempered;"

(Furthermore, six people were at the meeting.)

(So each person came from a different section of the alliance.)

(Thoughts that objected to authority were very strong in four sections of the alliance. The thoughts, however, differed from one another.)

whu melu ru sano lue ta shalimo phanoi miona mua shareo thorui to ti meilo. shia pai maeli to nai.

(The person from one section of the alliance whom the friend knew very well reportedly sat at the beginning of the discussion. They were almost silent.)
morris: "One of the sections, says our friend, a man whom he knows very well indeed, sat almost silent at the beginning of the discussion,"

whekai shia pheo laeno shemu muo shareo to se natu.
CONTR 3SG POST long moment INTO discuss PST PASS pull.
(After a long moment, however, they were pulled into the discussion.)
morris: "but at last got drawn into it,"

shia mua shareo lumae to ru theisa kapura.
3SG LOC discuss end PST INTS loud shout.
(At the end of the discussion they shouted very loudly.)
morris: "and finished by roaring out very loud,"

shia roe thiku nila tha theula phirae miona tawimo nai tho to ru keloa haolu.
3SG INS small see DECL.COMP UNIV different person foolish be DECL.COMP.CLOSE PST INTS rough speak.
(With contempt, they said very roughly that all the others were foolish.)
morris: "and damning all the rest for fools;"
```

**Notes:** the source calls four of the represented sections Anarchist. The historical label stays in Morris's line. Phi describes the opinions as objections to `karami`, authority, which says what they concern without pretending that every form of anarchism fits one dictionary sentence. The views belong to four sections, not four particular people, and their strength and mutual divergence are explicit.

The friend reports that the discussion stays good-tempered in spite of its subject. `Shai` carries the concession, and `noeli` describes emotional warmth rather than temperature. A second `shai` grants that the company did not listen to each other, and `soli shemu` beside a negated verb gives Morris's "did not always"; the assumptive claim then concedes that nobody could reasonably have expected better. Morris's dry "ordinary polite society" becomes `nawea punoa`, respectful society; custom `keno` supplies the ordinary part. The room does not immediately improve its case.

The voice that finally rises was not loud from the start. The man sits at `shareo thorui`, the discussion's beginning, and a sentence of its own says that he was `pai maeli`, nearly silent. He comes `lue ta shalimo phanoi`, from one section of the alliance, which keeps Morris's political structure without turning the man himself into a section. Contrastive `whekai` opens the change, `pheo laeno shemu` lets the long interval pass, and past passive `to se natu` keeps the sense that he was drawn in.

The outburst is `ru theisa kapura`, a forceful shout at great volume, at the discussion's end. The established composition `thiku nila` reports the contempt in Morris's "damning"; its parts say "see as small." `Ru keloa` makes the speech very harsh. This distinction lets Phi report contempt without adopting the man's judgement. Peaceful speech does not require a spotless narrator.

The headcount and its consequence occupy separate sentences. Additive `sheno` opens the census, and `thelao` links it to what follows. Each person comes from a different `shalimo phanoi`, alliance section. The six sections are therefore present without `shalori`, a verb for people who act on behalf of others, not ideas that happen to be in a room.

```
korua thero shia to ki pilu. ha wei shia we keno to nai.

(Anger had taken hold of them. For them this was also a custom.)
morris: "for having lost his temper (which he was also well used to),"

shia shareo to si themio.
3SG discuss PST IPFV reflect.
(They were reflecting on the discussion.)
morris: "he found himself musing on the subject-matter of discussion,"

whekai shia nuloe to si ma nai. shia mua korua nuhe to si phaelo.
CONTR 3SG content PST IPFV NEG be. 3SG LOC heart sadness PST IPFV feel.
(However, they were not content. They were feeling sadness in their heart.)
morris: "but still discontentedly and unhappily."

shia wei miso sha su mia wireo punoa ta philo li nila. su mia shia li nila sho to haolu.
3SG DAT REFL QUOT.COMP OPT 1SG future society one day RESTR see. OPT 1SG 3SG RESTR see QUOT.COMP.CLOSE PST speak.
(They said to themself, "May I only see one day of the future society. May I only see it.")
morris: "\"If I could but see a day of it,\" he said to himself; \"if I could but see it!\""

whau shia lo phelui to norelu nurako rolutha mua shia nurako lokue to te wepu.
SIMUL 3SG PL word PST form railway wagon LOC 3SG railway place PST CESS go.
(While they formed the words, the railway car stopped at their station.)
morris: "As he formed the words, the train stopped at his station,"

thalo ruela lue nurako lokue kau shia womu teku to nai. shia womu sio luphore to nai. shia leo whu mioru ma nai whu roe lo suro se panoru repha la wuero to nai.
walk path ABL railway place ALL 3SG home short PST be. 3SG home BESIDE river PST be. 3SG ABOVE REL beautiful NEG be REL INS PL rope PASS support bridge ATTEN far PST be.
(The walking path from the station to their home was short. Their home was beside the river. It was a little way above a bridge that was not beautiful and was held up by ropes.)
morris: "five minutes' walk from his own house, which stood on the banks of the Thames, a little way above an ugly suspension bridge."
```

**Notes:** `pheo` holds the outburst as the earlier event (`pheo shia ha to haolu`, after he had said this), and the period of noise follows it. The quiet interval needs no relator, since narrative order already puts it after the noise. During that lull the man explicitly says `pao`, goodbye, to his friends, and `ru noeli` makes the farewell very warm. `sonu` modifies his going, so Morris says that he travelled by himself rather than that he was otherwise alone. His home is in `whalo silawo sileta lumae phanoi`, the western part of the great town: enough to place a suburb without asking Phi for London's administrative vocabulary.

Morris calls railway travel something civilisation has forced upon "us." Phi keeps that pronoun: society coerced `lo mia`, and the railway journey became habitual. The man then sits in a carriage of `whu phou muila wepu nurako`, the railway that runs below the earth. Morris's vapour-bath survives as `sulae heshowa sawa lokue`, a warm vapour-washing place. Hurried and discontented people crowd around him, and he shares both their heat and their discontent.

The forgotten points are `themore`, arguments, judged `ru welao nela kithela`, very good and valid. `Nupira` is a feeling, so `whau ... phaelo` lets him feel self-reproach while he reflects. Morris leaves the arguments ready at the ends of the man's fingers; concessive `shai` then admits that he forgot them anyway in the `noshi shareo`, recent discussion. His familiar mood lasts only a short time. Disgust with himself causes a brief uneasiness, and `korua thero shia to ki pilu`, heart-fire had taken him. Negated `nuloe` says that he is not content, while `mua korua ... phaelo` places the sadness in his heart without making the person his feeling.

The wish is the chapter's first direct quotation. First-person `mia` sits inside `sha ... sho`; `su` makes the longing audible and `wei miso` gives it its only listener. Both cries sound, with the second pointing back to the society through `shia`. The train stops as he forms the words. A short walking path leads from the station to his home beside the river, a little above a bridge supported by ropes. Morris supplies the five-minute measure, the engineering label, and the names Thames and London in the source witness.


**Notes:** the station is `nurako lokue`, the railway's place. Outside it, the two states from the carriage return: the man is not content, and he still feels sadness in his heart. His muttered wish repeats in full. The walk then turns his state. Before he has gone far towards the river, his discontented mood and its heaviness seem to fall from him. The report matrix carries Morris's parenthesis, while inner `ke` marks "seemed" rather than declaring an inward change the narrator cannot inspect.

The night is at `shila thorui`, winter's beginning. After `sukaro tholupi`, the hot room, and the carriage `whu pothu`, which stinks, the air is `pe neri`, cool enough, and therefore fresh. Phi does not borrow the English sensory meaning of "sharp" for `tiso`, whose physical sense belongs to edges. The wind has lately turned a little from `sileta lumae`, the sun's end, towards north. Morris gives the exact points; Phi keeps the recent change and direction. Only one or two small cloud-flecks cross the cleared sky.

As the man goes home, `whau` makes his glimpse of the moon simultaneous with the walk rather than a separate event. The young moon hangs at `waero kesho`, the sky's middle, like a thing tied among the branches of a tall old elm. Its `limu` and the tree's `serao`, young and old, keep the quiet contrast in Morris's image. The worn, unbeautiful place is at the great town's edge, then almost drops out of memory. He feels like a person in pleasant country among fields, a place more pleasant than the distant countryside he has known. Morris's line gives London and its local category "suburb"; Phi does not turn either into universal geography.

**Notes:** arrival and lingering keep separate sentences. The arrival verb `kamo` takes `mua` because the event occurs at a place, while `kerime` names the river's shore. The man deliberately observes the `lunei keru luphore`, moon-bright river, over the low wall. It is near `raelu lunisa`, high tide, then turns `sorui`, in circles, towards the island and `keru tiripe`, quivers brightly. Phi gives Chiswick Eyot its island; Morris supplies the local name.

The bridge below the man is not beautiful, and ropes support it. This preserves the visible structure without misnaming the whole bridge as a rope. Morris gives it two denials, and Phi does too: he neither notices it nor thinks of it. One exception interrupts the calm for `ta shemu`, a single moment. Reportative `ti` returns because Morris inserts "says our friend." What strikes the man is the `holume`, loss, of a `luma sima`, a line or thread of lamps, `wea luphore lumae`, downstream towards the river's end.

The homecoming has three acts: he turns towards his door, lets himself in, and forgets as the door closes. The highly insightful, valid arguments and the foresight already named in the carriage vanish. `Lilea` says that these had greatly clarified the recent discussion; literal `keru`, brightness, does not borrow the source's cognitive use of light. Nothing remains except an unclear hope, which has now become pleasant, for days of peace, rest, cleanness, and kindness that smiles.


**Notes:** the comitative phrase `nua ha korua` carries the river walk's closing mood into the bedroom, and `lepa`, the same falling that took the heaviness from him, now tumbles him into bed. His usual quick sleep takes `phea keno`, while inchoative `pa` marks the onset of sleep rather than merely saying that he slept quickly. `Whuo keno` marks the unusual interruption. Morris supplies the exact two minutes in the source witness, while Phi says that he woke after a short moment. Direct `nowae` names waking rather than treating it as the mere cessation of sleep.

Morris then widens from one sleepless man to all of us. The condition is `kuewa`, strange, and `ru waeli`, thoroughly awake, even for a good sleeper. In this use, English "curiously" means strangely; `kuelo` would instead describe an inquisitive state. What makes the condition so unnerving follows: every `remori`, reasoning faculty, feels intensely sharp. Each miserable `remo tiwa`, confusion or thought-tangle, insists on presenting itself to that reason for consideration. One long coordination would turn `tiwa` into a predicate, so the shames and losses take their own sentence. The night appears to have kept records, though nobody recalls asking it to.

The lying awake carries another parenthetical "says our friend," so `ti` returns. He stays there until he has nearly begun to `kaeli`, enjoy, the state. He then becomes `monelu`, amused, at the story of his own foolish acts; `miso` marks the acts as his. His entanglements become tied threads that weave themselves into an amusing story. The bell is counted, not timed: `teli ta haoni`, `teli wi haoni`, `teli ta shao haoni`, one voice of the bell, then two, then three, each in its own sentence and with no clock hour named; Morris gives the exact hours in the source witness. `pheo shia ha to hea`, after he heard this, and inchoative `pa` bring the long night back to sleep. Mercifully, Morris does not ask the bell for a fourth opinion.

## The report continues

```
melu tha shia lue ra nulae we to nowae tho haolu.
friend DECL.COMP 3SG ABL DIST sleep ALSO PST awaken DECL.COMP.CLOSE speak.

morris: "Our friend says that from that sleep he awoke once more,"

morris: "and afterwards went through such surprising adventures"

morris: "and therefore proposes to tell them now."

whekai shia sha lu mia roe miso haoni lo ra shane. mia tha ha mo welao so nai tho remo. mia phea whu thue lo rulami to ki wepu wemi so haolu. mia sheo theula phirae muila miona ra wemi lo phaelo nela lo rinu mo shelomu. thelao phirae keiro sheo ha keiro wei mia mo kethua so nai. ha keiro wei mia mo woeli so nai sho haolu.
CONTR 3SG QUOT.COMP COND 1SG INS REFL voice PL DIST tell. 1SG DECL.COMP PROX CMPR good FUT be DECL.COMP.CLOSE think. 1SG AS REL THROUGH PL adventure PST PFV go ally FUT speak. 1SG THAN UNIV different earth person DIST ally PL feel COORD PL want CMPR understand. CONS different way THAN PROX way DAT 1SG CMPR hard FUT be. PROX way DAT 1SG CMPR natural FUT be QUOT.COMP.CLOSE speak.
(However, they say, "If I tell those in my own voice, I think this will be better. I will speak as the ally who has gone through the adventures. I understand that ally's feelings and wants better than every other person on earth does. So a different way will be harder for me than this way. This way will be more natural for me.")
morris: "But, says he, I think it would be better if I told them in the first person, as if it were myself who had gone through them; which, indeed, will be the easier and more natural to me, since I understand the feelings and desires of the comrade of whom I am telling better than any one else in the world does."
```

**Notes:** the report frame steps into view. The clause `melu tha ... tho haolu` says outright that the friend is speaking, just as Morris moves from parenthetical reminders to "Our friend says." He wakes from `ra nulae`, that sleep, and enters `lo rulami`, adventures that truly surprise. Passive necessity says they should be told, and `lo mia wemi` keeps Morris's inclusive "our comrades" before the audience widens to everyone. The verb `repora` keeps his proposal distinct from an intention.

The final quotation performs the handoff to the first person. Conditional `lu` keeps the better choice conditional: if he tells the story in his own voice, that will be better. The phrase `roe miso haoni`, with my own voice, states Morris's grammatical choice in ordinary words, and `wemi` identifies the person who went through the adventures as the ally in question. His feelings and desires share one comparison, against every other person in the world. Phi renders "easier" relationally by saying another way would be harder than this one, then states that this way would be more natural. From the next chapter onward, the report has become the traveller's own testimony.

## Translation limits

*The Phi follows every event and claim in Morris's chapter, including its political friction, unpleasant carriage, exacting self-reproach, and sly narrative handoff. Reportative `ti` keeps the friend's account visible. The repeated wish sits inside `sha ... sho` under `su`, and the last quotation changes the grammatical person in which the rest of the journey will be told.*

| Source detail | Phi treatment |
|---|---|
| The League and its political sections | `shalimo` names the continuing alliance, while `shalimo phanoi` names a section within it. Morris supplies the historical label *Anarchist*; Phi says that the opinions object to authority. |
| Civilisation and compulsory railway travel | `punoa` names the social order, `kawhera` states its coercion of the travellers, and `nurako` names the railway. |
| London's suburb, the Thames, and Chiswick Eyot | Phi places the house at the western edge of a great town, beside a river and above a bridge supported by ropes. Morris's wording supplies the local names and the engineering label *suspension bridge*. |
| Five minutes, compass points, and clock hours | Phi gives a short walk, a slight turn from west towards north, and three successive bell voices. The source lines supply the conventional measures. |
| Vapour-bath, swirling water, and a row of lamps | These compose as a warm vapour-washing place, circular turning with bright quivering, and a thread of lamps downstream. |
| First-person narration | `roe miso haoni`, with my own voice, states the narrator's choice; conditional `lu` makes that choice the condition for a better telling, and `phea` lets him speak as the ally who underwent the adventures. |

*No new root is required. The chapter draws freely on the Commons, Household, Philosophical Reasoning, and Systems modules where Morris's meeting, room, argument, and railway call for them. The modules earn their shelf space here: nobody should have to describe a railway carriage as merely a rather organised box.*
