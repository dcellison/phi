# The unmarked seams: study and rulings sheet

Date: 2026-11-24. Measured with `scripts/phi_readings.py` and `scripts/phi_roles.py` on the lexicon at 1,306 entries, before the ruling was implemented. This is the sheet the maintainer ruled from, kept as he read it, with the addendum written in answer to his follow-up question. His ruling on the six points in section 4, given the same day: "I agree with all of your recommendations." That is option B with the form `ku`. The addendum's question about subject and verb particles was not ruled on. Decision D134 records the result, and `canon.md` carries the rule.

Scope: the November assessment's central finding, section 4.3, and its recommendations 11, 13, and 31. Phi closes every embedded statement, question, and quotation with a word. It marks nothing where the subject ends and the object begins, where a prepositional phrase ends, or where a manner word could be an object. Reference (`shia`) is a separate sheet.

Any option that adds a word reopens the closed particle inventory. That decision is the maintainer's, and canon's Grammar Amendment Boundary sets the test: a repeatable defect shown in connected use, which existing grammar cannot resolve without structural ambiguity. Sections 1 and 2 are the showing.

## 1. The size of it

Counts are of the texts shelf (translations and originals together, about 3,250 sentences). A "division" is one way of cutting the words before the verb into subject, adjuncts, object, and manner. All figures are upper bounds on what the grammar leaves open; none is a rate of misreading.

| | Sentences | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| More than one division, counting only nouns, pronouns, and names as phrase ends | 1,388 | 43% |
| The same, also letting an event or quality noun head a phrase | about 2,170 | 67% |
| A noun or name directly beside a noun, edge open | about 650 | 20% |

The ambiguous clauses (1,684) sort by what is uncertain:

| What is uncertain | Clauses |
|---|---:|
| Where the object begins: after the subject, after a possessor, or after a prepositional phrase | 1,328 |
| Where the subject ends before a time word or an adjunct, with no object in play | 198 |
| How the words before a fixed object divide | 157 |

Two smaller kinds sit beside these. In 197 clauses of the translations one bare phrase stands before a verb that takes an object, so the phrase is subject or object. And across the whole corpus about one adverbial clause in five (66 of 316) and one relative clause in five (53 of 268) has more than one grammatical end.

## 2. What readers do with an unmarked seam

Fourteen short sentences were written so that both readings make sense. Two fresh readers saw them unmarked, with only the Phi, a gloss, and the entries. Each gave a first reading, the other readings, and a confidence that the first was meant.

| Phi | First reading | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| `ne samira tuleno haoni to hea.` | both: Samira heard the frog's voice | 80%, 80% |
| `phao lopia shelu theo.` | both: the parent reads the child's book | 50%, 45% |
| `melu lohau nuora to nuola.` | both: the friend's dog ate the food | 60%, 60% |
| `lohau mua thepalu to nai. lopia to nila.` | both: the child saw it | 65%, 60% |
| `tupiwa tha lo phirae tupiwa so ma nila tho to soliru.` | one: it would not see the other rabbits; the other: the other rabbits would not see it | 60%, 55% |
| `whu mia nila miona to shua.` | both: the person I see came | 70%, 75% |
| `lopia roe phao kiru wolea to kati.` | both: cut the wood with the parent's chisel | 85%, 88% |
| `shia welao thumela.` | both: they teach well | 60%, 70% |
| `mia nohero to helui.` | both: my belief changed | 50%, 50% |
| `wheo lopia nophi to shane.` | both: the elder told the child's story | 45%, 40% |
| `phao lopia shelu ka theo.` | both: the parent has the child read the book | 75%, 70% |
| `melu lopia to naphe.` | both: the friend helped the child | 65%, 65% |

Three things stand out.

- The readers mostly agree with each other, and neither is sure. Mean confidence on these twelve is 63%.
- The split is not consistent. The same three-noun shape is cut after the first noun in two sentences and after the second in a third, by plausibility alone.
- The fifth sentence is the certified *Velveteen Rabbit* line the assessment found misread. Given a neutral subject, two readers took opposite roles for `lo phirae tupiwa`.

## 3. The options

### A. Teach the repairs that exist, and add nothing

The assessment's step 2. Move a possessor into a relative clause (`whu phao phelu shelu`, the book the parent holds); keep the subject explicit; put a Slot 1 particle in so that manner and object separate; say that a careful speaker breaks slightly between phrases.

- Costs no inventory and can be written today.
- The possessor repair adds two words (`whu ... phelu`) at each use. The Slot 1 repair is not available in the plain present. Pauses do not survive on the page, and canon says they cannot change meaning.
- It does not help the 197 open roles: a lone phrase before `nila` stays subject or object.

### B. One optional word that opens the object phrase (recommended)

A Slot 2 particle, said only when the edge or the role needs saying. Everything from the particle to the Slot 1 stack or the verb is the object; whatever stands before it is subject or adjunct. The unmarked clause stays the resting state, as the unmarked sentence does for evidence.

With the placeholder form `ku`:

| Phi | Reading |
|---|---|
| `phao ku lopia shelu theo.` | the parent reads the child's book |
| `phao lopia ku shelu theo.` | the parent's child reads the book |
| `ku lopia to nila.` | (it) saw the child |
| `whu ku mia nila miona` | the person who sees me |
| `shia ku welao thumela.` | they teach goodness |
| `mia ku nohero to helui.` | I changed the belief |

**Simulated reach.** In 1,328 of the 1,684 ambiguous clauses (79%) every reading has its object beginning at a different word, so the particle alone tells them apart. 1,006 of the 1,388 ambiguous sentences (72%) would be settled completely. It also settles every open role and every open relative head, which no boundary marker can.

**Reading test.** The same fourteen sentences went to two other fresh readers with `ku` placed to select one reading, usually the one the unmarked readers had not chosen. Its entry was six sentences long.

| Phi | Both readers |
|---|---|
| `ne samira tuleno ku haoni to hea.` | Samira's frog heard a voice |
| `phao lopia ku shelu theo.` | the parent's child reads the book |
| `melu ku lohau nuora to nuola.` | the friend ate the dog's food |
| `... ku lopia to nila.` | it saw the child |
| `tupiwa tha ku lo phirae tupiwa so ma nila tho to soliru.` | it would not see the other rabbits |
| `whu ku mia nila miona to shua.` | the person who sees me came |
| `lopia roe phao kiru ku wolea to kati.` | cut the wood with the parent's chisel |
| `shia ku welao thumela.` | they teach goodness |
| `mia ku nohero to helui.` | I changed the belief |
| `wheo ku lopia nophi to shane.` | the elder told the child's story |
| `ku melu lopia to naphe.` | (someone) helped the friend's child |

All eleven were read as marked by both readers, and each reader ruled the other reading out in so many words ("not available as written", "not licensed"). In eight of them the marker turned the reading away from what the unmarked readers had chosen. Mean confidence rose from 63% to 78%. What doubt remained was of other kinds: whether a noun before a noun owns it or names its kind (dog's food, dog food), and who an unsaid subject is.

One sentence exposed a gap in the rule. In the causative `phao lopia ku shelu ka theo.` both readers chose "the parent has the child read the book" and both noted that, read strictly, everything before `ku` would be one subject (the parent's child). A ruling has to say where the person made to act stands. See point 3.

**Why an object word and not a bare boundary word.** Languages that mark objects only when it matters are common: Spanish *a*, Hebrew *et*, Persian *-râ*, Turkish, and the object particle Japanese speakers drop in conversation. The pattern exists because subject and object are the pair most worth keeping apart. It also fits the organizing principle: the relation is announced before the phrase it governs.

**Costs.**

- A thirty-ninth particle, in the slot that is already the fullest.
- Phi has had no marking of roles at all. This is the first, even though optional.
- If a writer marked every clause where it could matter, about one clause in three on the texts shelf would carry it, close to one word in twenty-three. `to` is one word in fourteen. Optional use will be far lower, and how much lower is a matter of habit that the ledger will show.
- It does nothing for 355 clauses where the uncertainty is before the object or without one, mostly a time word after the subject (`ne lopia ta howai kau nuwera to wepu`, whose other reading is "Lopia's one evening went to bed"), nor for the open clause ends.

### C. One optional word that opens any phrase

The assessment's recommendation 31. The same particle, without a role: it may stand before the object, before a bare time phrase, or before the subject of a main clause after a dependent one.

- Reaches further than B on boundaries: with enough markers every division is distinct, including the 355 clauses B leaves and the open clause ends.
- Says nothing about roles. `X lopia to nila` would open a phrase and leave it subject or object.
- A sentence may need two or three, where B needs one.
- A word that means only "a phrase starts here" has no parallel elsewhere in Phi, whose particles all say what they are opening.

### D. An optional possessive linker

A word between possessor and possessed, so that an unmarked pair of nouns is two phrases. It marks the inside of a phrase where B marks its edge.

- Settles the noun chains as well as C does.
- Nothing for roles, for manner or object, or for the end of a prepositional phrase without a second marker.
- It lands on Phi's most characteristic habit, the noun before a noun, and on every entry in the compound registry. Each compound would become a choice between two forms.

### E. `ha` as a phrase opener

Closed already. The maintainer ruled on 2026-11-19 that Phi has no articles. For the record, treating `ha`, `ra`, and `lo` as always opening a phrase would lower the share of ambiguous sentences in the translations from 42% to 35%.

## 4. Points to rule on

**Point 1. Which option.** Recommended: **B**, with the repairs in A taught beside it, since they cost nothing and cover cases where a speaker prefers to restructure.

**Point 2. The form, if B or C.** Eight plain one-syllable forms are free. The word will stand where `ha`, `ra`, `lo`, `ko`, and the numerals stand, so those are its nearest rivals.

| Form | Nearest words in the same position | English echo | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ku` | `ko` (focus), `nu`, `ru` | "coo" | Recommended. Its other `k` neighbours (`ka`, `ke`, `ki`) are all Slot 1 and stand before verbs. |
| `hu` | `ha` (this), `nu`, `ru` | "who" | `ha` is very frequent before nouns. Canon kept `who` out for how it reads in English. |
| `re` | `ra` (that), `ne`, `we`, `pe`, `le` | "ray" | Five Slot 2 words already end in `e`. |
| `tu` | `ta` (one), `nu`, `ru` | "two" | Sounds like English "two" while Phi's two is `wi`, and stands where `ta` stands. |
| `ri` | `li` (only) | | `l` against `r` is the contrast most learners lose. |
| `mi` | `mia` (I) | "me" | One vowel short of the first person; also the retired superlative. |
| `me` | `ne`, `we`, `pe`, `mo` | "meh" | Crowded. |
| `pu` | `pe` | "poo" | |

The form is for the maintainer's ear. `ku` was used in the test only as a placeholder.

**Point 3. What it marks, if B.** Recommended:

- The direct object phrase, and nothing else. A complement of `nai` or `kelu` is not an object and never takes it.
- It works the same inside a relative clause.
- In a causative, it marks the thing the caused act falls on. The person made to act stands bare between the subject and the particle: `phao lopia ku shelu ka theo`. If that person and the subject could run together, the subject is the one that needs repair, by a relative clause or a name.
- It is never required. A clause without it is complete and ordinary.

**Point 4. Where it stands in its phrase, if B.** Recommended: at the outer edge, before `we` and `li` and before any possessor: `ku we lopia shelu` (also the child's book, as object). That keeps one rule: the object starts at the particle.

**Point 5. What B leaves.** The time word after a subject and the open clause ends. Recommended: add nothing for these. The time-word reading ("Lopia's one evening") is grammatical nonsense that readers set aside without help; both unmarked readers did. Teach the existing repair for a clause end, which is to begin the main clause with something that cannot be owned: a pronoun or a name. Watch both on the ledger.

**Point 6. Whether the texts must use it.** Recommended: no rule. The word is optional by design. Once it exists, a translation's certification can ask the source-blind reader's doubts to decide where it is needed, which is the test that matters.

## Appendix: limits of the evidence

- The simulation asks whether an object word could tell a clause's readings apart. It does not know which reading a writer meant, or whether the writer would have bothered.
- The reading test is four model readers, two per condition, on fourteen invented sentences built to be reversible. Real text is less evenly balanced, and context settles much of it. The test shows that an unmarked seam is read on plausibility with middling confidence, and that a six-sentence entry was enough for the marker to be read correctly every time.
- The unmarked readers' entry for `nila` and the grammar notes were the ones in the repository. Both unmarked readers and both marked readers flagged the bare time phrase `ta howai` as possibly unlicensed, which is a gap in the packet's notes and not in the grammar.

## Addendum: optional subject and verb particles

Asked by the maintainer after reading the sheet: Phi began with optional markers for subject, object, and verb, so should the object word be joined by the other two? The 2021 and 2022 particle pages list `si` (subject, optional, always sentence-initial), `na` (object), and `te` (verb). All three forms now do other work (`si` imperfective, `na` necessity, `te` cessative).

What each would settle, simulated on the 1,388 ambiguous sentences of the texts shelf:

| Markers available | Ambiguous sentences settled | Left ambiguous, share of all text sentences |
|---|---:|---:|
| object | 72% | 11.8% |
| subject alone | 1% | 42% |
| verb alone | 2% | 42% |
| object and subject | 75% | 11% |
| object and verb | 75% | 11% |
| object, subject, and verb | 78% | 10% |
| object, plus an existing preposition on a bare time phrase | 89% | 4.8% |
| that, and subject | 92% | 3.5% |
| that, subject, and verb | 95% | 2.2% |

What each is for:

- A subject particle shows where a main clause begins after a dependent clause (50 open adverbial ends in the texts) and lets a lone phrase be asserted as subject (214 open roles). An object particle already separates the two roles from the other side.
- A verb particle separates manner from object where no Slot 1 particle stands (62 clauses in the texts) and shows where a relative clause ends (53). In two clauses of every three a Slot 1 particle already marks the verb; 34% of clauses in the texts have none.
- Neither touches the largest remainder after the object word, which is a bare time phrase after the subject. Existing grammar settles that: `mua` or `sui` before the time phrase.

A verb particle would be, in effect, an optional present-tense particle, the unfilled place beside `to` and `so`.
