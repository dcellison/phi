# The core pronouns

Say them in a row: *mi-a*, *thi-a*, *shi-a*, *hi-a*. One shape, four onsets. Phi hands every person in a conversation a word of the same size, two syllables on the same open hiatus, and who you are in the sentence changes the consonant and nothing else.

Phi has four singular personal pronouns: one for the speaker, one for the addressee, and two for everyone else. They mark grammatical person without gender, animacy, rank, or case endings.

| Phi | Gloss | Referent |
|---|---|---|
| **mia** | 1SG | the speaker |
| **thia** | 2SG | the addressee |
| **shia** | 3SG | one other being or thing |
| **hia** | 3ALT | the other of two such beings or things |

## mia: the speaker

**mia** keeps the same form in every role:

**mia thia nila.** — I see you.

**thia mia sano.** — You know me.

**mia shea lothea.** — I love peace.

In careful speech, **mia** has two syllables: stressed **mi**, then a separate unstressed **a**. The same hiatus pattern unites all four core pronouns.

## thia: the addressee

**thia** refers to one person or thing being addressed:

**wa thia shea nai.** — Are you at peace?

**mia thia whaline.** — I thank you.

**thia womu lothea.** — You love home.

Second person has one form across formal and informal settings. Politeness, when wanted, belongs to **pi**, and named relationships may use an honorific. The single form declines to encode rank, so the grammar never asks who outranks whom before you can say you. Its `th` asks the tongue for a moment of deliberate placement, and some speakers like it that the word for you begins with a sound that cannot be said carelessly.

## shia: another being or thing

**shia** refers to one third-person being or thing:

**shia shua.** — They arrive. It arrives.

**mia shia sano.** — I know them. I know it.

**shia womu mioru nai.** — Their home is beautiful.

The pronoun marks neither gender nor animacy. English may render it as singular *they*, *he*, *she*, or *it* according to the referent. A noun or productive name can identify someone more specifically when it matters; the pronoun's whole ambition is to let a person be mentioned without being categorized.

## hia: the other one

A story with one other person in it needs only **shia**. Put a second person in and the word starts to wobble:

```
wheo wei lopia to haolu. shia to hola.
elder DAT child PST speak. 3SG PST laugh.
(The elder spoke to the child. One of them laughed.)
```

The grammar does not say who laughed, and a listener picks whoever the scene favors. **hia** says it was the other one, the one who was not the subject a moment ago:

```
wheo wei lopia to haolu. hia to hola.
elder DAT child PST speak. 3ALT PST laugh.
(The elder spoke to the child. The child laughed.)
```

Inside a single clause the reference point is that clause's own subject. **miso** is the subject again; **hia** is someone else:

**shia miso womu to nila.** — They saw their own home.

**shia hia womu to nila.** — They saw the other one's home.

When **hia** owns the subject, the clause has no subject of its own yet for it to differ from, so it looks back one sentence, as a subject does:

```
phao nua lopia to thalo. hia manuwe neri to nai.
parent COM child PST walk. 3ALT hand cool PST be.
(The parent walked with the child. The child's hand was cool.)
```

**hia** goes wherever **shia** goes and takes **lo** for more than one. It says nothing about what kind of being it points at. Nobody has to use it: **shia** stays the ordinary word and leans on context, and a name settles anything. **hia** is for the sentence where context would lean the wrong way.

It keeps two apart and no more. When a third person walks into the scene, **hia** can no longer say which other, and a name does the work.

## Case comes from position

The forms do not change as subject, object, possessor, or prepositional object:

| Role | Phi | English |
|---|---|---|
| subject | **mia thia nila.** | I see you. |
| object | **thia mia nila.** | You see me. |
| prepositional object | **thia wei mia shua.** | You come to me. |
| possessor | **mia shelu welao nai.** | My book is good. |

A bare pronoun at the start of a predicate clause is the subject. Predicative possession uses **phelu**, as chapter 11 explains.

## Topic-drop

Once a subject is established and remains recoverable, a later clause may omit it:

```
shai shelira thiku nai mioru nai.
CONC forest small be beautiful be.
(Although the forest is small, it is beautiful.)
```

```
mia shelu theo. shelomu.
1SG book read. understand.
(I read the book. I understand.)
```

An unsaid subject is the subject of the sentence before. In a story that makes the dropped subject the firmest choice a teller has, because it can only mean that the same one went on:

```
ne sulae mua nuwera to meilo. wei woru to haolu.
NAME sulae LOC bed PST sit. DAT keeper PST speak.
(Sulae sat in bed and spoke to the keeper.)
```

Topic-drop is optional. Repeating the noun or pronoun is always available when two referents might compete.

## Who is meant

Five devices say who is meant, and each looks in a different place:

| Phi | Who it is |
|---|---|
| an unsaid subject | the subject of the sentence before |
| **miso** | the subject of this clause |
| **hia** | someone other than that subject |
| **shia** | whoever the context favors |
| a noun or a name | exactly the one it names |

Most sentences need none of this care. One person is in view, **shia** means them, and the story moves. The other four are for the moment a second person steps in and either of them could have laughed.
